Convergence Point

Why my wave simulation kept blowing up

A few years ago, I spent a while implementing some simulations of massless scalar fields in AdS. This means that we were using the simplest description of matter with only a Kinetic term in a geometric background with a negative cosmological constant.

Why is this interesting?

Although these collections of spacetimes are not favored by measurements made of our current Universe, it has been shown that the dynamics in the AdS spacetime is related to conformal field theories, as in what happens in one system has an analogous phenomenon in the other. People have been using this relationship to better understand both classes of systems.

The starting point for what I'm simulating is described in a technical paper (Bizoń & Rostworowski, arXiv:1104.3702) but I'll explain here anyway.


Einstein-scalar field equations (massless scalar, \(\Lambda < 0\) ):

\begin{equation} G_{\alpha\beta} + \Lambda g_{\alpha\beta} = 8\pi G\left(\partial_\alpha\phi\,\partial_\beta\phi - \tfrac{1}{2}\,g_{\alpha\beta}(\partial\phi)^2\right) \end{equation} \begin{equation} g^{\alpha\beta}\nabla_\alpha\nabla_\beta\,\phi = 0 \end{equation}

Spherically symmetric metric ansatz, with \(\ell^2=-3/\Lambda\) and \(0\le x<\pi/2\):

\begin{equation} ds^2 = \frac{\ell^2}{\cos^2 x}\left(-A e^{-2\delta}\,dt^2 + A^{-1}dx^2 + \sin^2\!x\,d\Omega^2\right) \end{equation}

Wave equation, with \(\Phi=\phi'\) and \(\Pi=A^{-1}e^{\delta}\dot\phi\) (dots and primes denote \(\partial_t\) and \(\partial_x\)):

\begin{equation} \dot\Phi = \left(A e^{-\delta}\,\Pi\right)', \qquad \dot\Pi = \frac{1}{\tan^2 x}\left(\tan^2 x\; A e^{-\delta}\,\Phi\right)' \end{equation}

Constraints (units where \(4\pi G=1\)):

\begin{equation} A' = \frac{1+2\sin^2 x}{\sin x\cos x}\,(1-A) - \sin x\cos x\,A\left(\Phi^2+\Pi^2\right) \end{equation} \begin{equation} \delta' = -\sin x\cos x\left(\Phi^2+\Pi^2\right) \end{equation}

To clarify what this all means:
  • Eq. 1 is the Einstein equation, the left-hand side is the spacetime geometry and the right-hand side is the energy-momentum tensor given the scalar field's interactions.
  • Eq. 2 is the scalar field evolution equations.
  • Eq. 3 is the metric tensor. This tensor tells us how distances/times change with coordinates.
  • Eq. 4 are both a trivial result of the change of variables we are using and a derivation from Eq. 2. These equations are at the center of our simulation as they define how the field evolves.
  • Eq. 5 and Eq. 6 are derived from a lengthy computation from Eq. 1 with the metric tensor in Eq. 3. These tell us the profiles of how the space curves with the presence of the field.
All of these functions are dependent on the (t,x) coordinates.
Regarding the metric tensor... In these coordinates, we can visualize the spacetime as a cylinder where x is the radius of the cylinder and t the "height". The solution we are looking for is spherically symmetric so we are looking for variations in the dt.dt and dr.dr components. Notice also that the impact of the cosmological constant is absorbed into the coordinate system: this means that if we study the system for any given value of the cosmological constant, we'll be able to scale the solution for another value without redoing the whole simulation/calculations.

Now, if you look at eq. 4 and squint your eyes a little, you'll be able to see the wave equation in spherical coordinates. If A = 1 and delta = 0 we have

\[ \dot\Pi = \frac{1}{\tan^2 x}\,\bigl(\tan^2 x\,\Phi\bigr)' \]

This is the massless wave equation on the AdS background in spherical coordinates with spherical symmetry and m=0, where tan²x plays the role that r² plays in flat space. The massless Klein-Gordon equation on pure AdS, which has the same structure as the spherical wave equation.

Now that we know how to compute the profiles and field evolution we need to understand how they behave at the boundaries. There are two important boundaries: x = 0 and x = pi/2. For the system to be physically representative we need to set boundary conditions

Boundary behavior of \(\phi\), \(A\) and \(\delta\), from Bizoń and Rostworowski, arXiv:1104.3702.

Near the origin (\(x\to 0\)), where smoothness gives power series in \(x\), normalized so that \(\delta(t,0)=0\) and \(t\) is the proper time at the center:

\begin{equation}\tag{7} \begin{aligned} \phi(t,x) &= f_0(t) + O(x^2),\\ \delta(t,x) &= O(x^2),\\ A(t,x) &= 1 + O(x^2). \end{aligned} \end{equation}

Near the AdS boundary (\(x\to\pi/2\)), with \(\rho=\pi/2-x\), where smoothness and finite total mass \(M\) fix the expansions:

\begin{equation}\tag{8} \begin{aligned} \phi(t,x) &= f_\infty(t)\,\rho^3 + O(\rho^5),\\ \delta(t,x) &= \delta_\infty(t) + O(\rho^6),\\ A(t,x) &= 1 - 2M\rho^3 + O(\rho^6). \end{aligned} \end{equation}



Solving the equation

For the simulation, we need to discretize the spacetime. We use a finite differences method and for the time evolution we use RK4.

We set a grid with N equidistant points over the radial coordinate ranging from 0 to pi/2. In this case we'll have
\[ dx = \frac{\pi}{2(N-1)} \]

Likewise we also have a condition for the timestep we are able to calculate. The wave velocity in these coordinates is lesser or equal to 1 and the Courant condition requires
\[ dt \leqslant \frac{dx}{c} = dx \]

In practice, I use dt = dx/2.
At any given time we can compute the value of A and δ and then use them to compute the rhs of the evolution equations. At the boundaries I add ghost points outside the grid, with values set by the parity of Π and Φ at each end, so the stencil can be applied at every grid point.

So... What's the challenge here?

If you take a look at the evolution equation for Π, we see that we have, near the x=0, an indetermination 0/0 and we need to be careful about how we handle this.

Expanding the product gives
\[\dot\Pi = \Phi' + \frac{2\Phi}{\sin x\cos x} \]
and the coefficient blows up at both ends. At the origin there is a clean limit: Φ = O(x), so Φ/(sin x cos x) → Φ′(0) and
\[ \dot\Pi(0) = 3\Phi'(0) \]
That handles the 0/0. It does not make the scheme stable. This form is unstable for every time step I tried. The worst mode grows at a rate of about 0.39/dx near π/2 and 0.1/dx near 0, because that is where 1/(sin x cos x) ≈ 1/dx. This means that the more we discretize the grid to finer results, the larger the growth rate of this instability near the edges.

Why it happens.

The continuous system conserves
\[ E=\int \tan^2 x\,(\Pi^2+\Phi^2)\,dx \]
but only because of the product rule:
\[ (\tan^2 x\,\Phi)' = \tan^2 x \Phi' + (\tan^2 x)' \Phi \]
A finite-difference stencil does not obey the product rule. For smooth data the defect is tiny (about 1e-5 or less). For grid-scale oscillations it is of order 1, and those are exactly the modes that grow. Splitting the product feeds energy into them.

A first attempt is to not split the product. Build g = tan²x·A·e^{-δ}·Φ, differentiate it, and divide afterwards. This converges at order 4 and looks stable on short runs. It isn't. The origin node has energy weight tan²(0) = 0 yet still couples to its neighbours, which leaves a growing mode that only appears after t ≈ 100 to 300. In the full Einstein system it can look like a collapse before the run turns into NaN. A short run is not a stability test.The actual fix is to absorb tan x into the fields, Θ = tan x·Π and Ψ = tan x·Φ (with an extra factor √(A e^{-δ}) in the full system). Then
\[ \dot\Theta = \Psi' + b\Psi,\qquad \dot\Psi = \Theta' - b\Theta,\qquad b=\frac{1}{\sin x\cos x} \]
The b terms have opposite signs, so they cancel pointwise in the energy and no product rule is needed. The spatial operator conserves the discrete energy exactly, and its eigenvalues are purely imaginary. With this form the runs stay stable for much longer.

The results

We start with a Gaussian initial condition on Π and flat Φ.
We let the fields evolve and the pulse reaches x=π/2 at t = π/2 and returns to the origin at t = π, so the dynamics has period π. If the field is intense enough, the backreaction causes the field to focus on an increasingly narrower band of space, eventually collapsing into a black hole. In the simulation below there's the evolution of one such example. We determine that a black hole is formed when the function A goes to 0, with the initial BH horizon at the position where A went to 0.

In the simulation A doesn't go all the way to 0 because the velocity of the wave goes to 0 and, along with the metric ansatz,  our implementation is no longer able to continue the computation. We are choosing A < 1/N as the threshold. The only reason we don't see it go that far in the video is because we only save every M frames and the simulation went for a few more frames before that condition was actually met.

For more results, feel free to check the original technical paper linked at the beginning.

As a final personal note, I'm proud of myself. After so much time and having this ghost of not knowing what went wrong hovering over me. I feel it brings some kind of closure.

I hope this helps anyone that finds themselves with similar problems solving numerically variants of the wave equations in spherical coordinates.
