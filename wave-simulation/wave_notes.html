1. Setup

The fields are Π=φ˙ and Φ=φ′, with x∈[0,π/2]. A dot is ∂t and a prime is ∂x.

Notation used below. a≡tan⁡x, w≡a2, b≡1sin⁡xcos⁡x=a′a, m≡Ae−δ (m=1 on the fixed AdS background). The grid spacing is h=π/2n (dr in the code) and the time step is Δt=h/2.

The linear equations on the fixed AdS background are

Φ˙=Π′ Π˙=1w(wΦ)′

Boundary behaviour

  • At x=0: Π is even and Φ is odd.
  • Near x=π/2: φ=f(t)ρ3+O(ρ5) with ρ=π/2−x. So Π is odd and Φ is even.
  • Singular limits are filled explicitly. At x=0, Φ/x→Φ′ gives Π˙=3Φ′. At x=π/2, wΦ has a finite limit, extrapolated from three interior points (even polynomial in ρ).

Numerical choices

  • Δt=h/2. The characteristic speed is 1 in x, so this is stable for the linear problem.
  • Initial data: Π(0,x)=amplitudeπe−a2/σ2, Φ(0,x)=0, σ=1/16.
  • Initial data must be smooth under the boundary reflections. A nonzero Π′(0), or a tail at x=π/2, produces a kink after reflection and lowers the measured order.

The metric decides the equation. In flat space with r=La one gets

Π˙=cos4⁡xL2(Φ′+2cot⁡xΦ)

whereas in AdS4 the equation is the one above, with w=tan2x.

2. Stencils and the product rule

A finite-difference stencil does not obey the product rule exactly. Take the centred difference (Df)i=fi+1−fi−12h and apply it to a product:

(D(uv))i =ui+1vi+1−ui−1vi−12h ui(Dv)i+vi(Du)i =ui(vi+1−vi−1)+vi(ui+1−ui−1)2h

These differ. Let a+=ui+1−ui and a−=ui−ui−1, with b± the same differences of v. The defect is

D(uv)−(uDv+vDu)=a+b+−a−b−2h

a product of two differences divided by h.

  • Smooth data: the differences are O(h), so the defect is O(h2). It shrinks with the grid, which is why it is invisible on smooth data (1e-5 to 1e-9 relative here).
  • Grid-scale oscillations such as (−1)i: the differences are as large as the values, and the defect is of order 1 (0.6 to 2.2 measured). These are the modes that grow.
Why it matters. The continuous system conserves E=∫w(Π2+Φ2)dx, because Π(wΦ)′+wΦΠ′=(wΠΦ)′. That identity is the product rule. The expanded form (wΦ)′=wΦ′+w′Φ assumes it, so the discrete energy is not conserved, and the error feeds the high-frequency modes.

The conservative form D(wΦ)/w never splits the product, so it has no defect. It is better than the expanded form, but it still fails in a subtler way (section 4).

Why not split the product anyway?

You can. The split (expanded) form is consistent: on smooth data its truncation error goes to zero with h at fixed t. What fails is stability, and by the Lax equivalence theorem consistency alone does not give convergence. The explanation takes four steps.

Step 1: what a stable scheme must preserve

The continuous system is Φ˙=∂Π, Π˙=−∂†Φ, where ∂†=−w−1∂w is the adjoint of ∂ in the w-weighted inner product (section 5). Energy is conserved because the second operator is exactly minus the adjoint of the first.

A scheme keeps that property if its two discrete operators keep the same relation in the discrete inner product ⟨f,g⟩=∑iHiwifigi. With summation by parts (DTH=−HD), the discrete adjoint of D is −W−1DW, with W=diag(wi). So the Π˙ operator that conserves energy is W−1DW, which is the conservative form D(wΦ)/w. The split form uses D+2B instead, with B=diag(bi) and w′/w=2b. (The conservative form has its own problem at the origin, where w0=0 makes the inner product degenerate; see section 4.)

Step 2: the mismatch of the split form

Write the stencil as (Df)i=∑kdkfi+k. The amount by which the split operator misses the energy-conserving one is

R≡(D+2B)−W−1DW, (Rf)i=2bifi+∑kdk(1−wi+kwi)fi+k

A concrete case makes this readable. Take the 2-point stencil, d±1=±1/(2h), and a weight that varies slowly, wi±1/wi≈1±2hbi. Then the sum collapses to

(Rf)i=2bifi−bi(fi+1+fi−1)=−bi(fi+1−2fi+fi−1)

So the mismatch is b times a second difference.

  • For smooth f the second difference is h2f″, so Rf is negligible. This is why the split form looks fine on resolved data.
  • For the sawtooth fi=(−1)i the second difference is −4fi, so Rf=4bf: not small at all. This is the product-rule defect of the previous subsection, now seen as an operator.

The code uses wider stencils, so the coefficients differ, but the structure is the same: a smooth part that cancels and a grid-scale part that does not. The 2-point form is an illustration, not a calculation for derP.

Step 3: from mismatch to growth, and why it scales like 1/h

In the weighted inner product the symmetric part of the system matrix is 120RR†0. The real part of any eigenvalue is at most the largest eigenvalue of the symmetric part, which is 12‖R‖. Energy-conserving schemes have R=0; here the growth is limited by the size of R, which is of order b.

The key point is the size of b=1/(sin⁡xcos⁡x):

  • In the interior b=O(1), so the mismatch gives at most an h-independent growth rate.
  • At the first cells next to the ends, b≈1/x≈1/h at x=h and b≈1/ρ≈1/h at ρ=h. The growth rate can be as large as O(1/h).

This agrees with what was measured: growth rates of about 0.1/h near 0 and 0.39/h near π/2, localized at the ends and growing as the grid is refined. Both are fractions of 1/h, consistent with an upper bound of order b. It also explains why patching the quotient near the ends (l'Hôpital, hybrid forms) only moved the growth: it changes b there but leaves the structure R≠0. As a rough scale, noise at 10−16 growing at 0.39/h reaches order 1 after t≈ln(1016)h/0.39≈0.3 for n=500. (This is arithmetic on the measured rate, not a separate run. The decomposition above is my own hand derivation; I did not compute R numerically for the code's stencils.)

Where b comes from, and why it limits stability

The function b was defined as the logarithmic derivative of a=tan⁡x:

b=a′a=1/cos2⁡xsin⁡x/cos⁡x=1sin⁡xcos⁡x=2sin⁡2x

Because w=a2, the weight's logarithmic derivative is w′/w=2a′/a=2b. That is where the term 2bΦ comes from when (wΦ)′/w is expanded into Φ′+(w′/w)Φ.

Meaning. b is half the relative rate of change of the weight. It says how much w changes from one point to the next, as a fraction of w itself.

Size of b
LocationLocal form of wb
Next to the origin, x=hw≈x2b≈1/x=1/h
Middle, x=π/4w=1b=2
Next to π/2, ρ=π/2−x=hw≈1/ρ2b≈1/ρ=1/h

For n=500 one has 1/h≈318, against b=2 in the middle.

Why it limits stability. In the 2-point illustration of step 2, the mismatch is −b times a second difference, so b is the coefficient of the term that does not cancel. The possible growth rate therefore follows b: of order 1 in the interior, and of order 1/h at the first cells next to the ends. This is a heuristic bound from a hand calculation, not a proof of the exact rate.

There is a second reason the end cells are special. The simplification in step 2 used wi±1/wi≈1±2hb, which holds only when hb is small. At the first cells hb≈1, so the weight changes by an order-1 factor between neighbouring points. For example, at i=1 the ratio is w2/w1≈tan2(2h)/tan2(h)≈4. The stencil cannot treat the weight as slowly varying there, which is why the instability sits at the ends and worsens as h shrinks.

This matches the measurements: the growth rates of about 0.1/h near 0 and 0.39/h near π/2 are fractions of the 1/h that b reaches at the first cells.

A mode of the semi-discrete operator with eigenvalue λ grows in amplitude like eReλt, so the growth rate is the largest Reλ of the operator. Step 4: the time stepper cannot fix it

The instability belongs to the spatial operator. RK4 integrates eλt accurately, so if Reλ>0 it reproduces the growth. Reducing Δt does not move the eigenvalues, which is why the expanded form blew up for every time step.

A split that works

Let M be the diagonal matrix of a coefficient m. In the inner product above, the adjoint of the one-sided operator MD is −DM, not −MD. So MD alone is not skew. The average is:

(12(MD+DM))†=12(−DM−MD)=−12(MD+DM)

This is the operator S of section 4. The proof uses only that D is skew in H and that M is diagonal. It never uses the product rule, which is why it works. The product rule would be the statement DM−MD=diag(Dm), and that is false for a stencil on grid-scale data. In the literature this is the skew-symmetric (split) form.

An alternative: add dissipation

One could keep the expanded form and add Kreiss-Oliger dissipation with a coefficient large enough to beat the growth near the ends. This was not tried in this code. It adds a tuning parameter and damps real high-frequency content. It also hides the symptom instead of fixing it, so the eigenvalue test would no longer show whether the operator itself is correct.

Why differentiating the product first works

The conservative form applies the stencil to the product and divides afterwards: Π˙=W−1D(WΦ), Φ˙=DΠ. It works because the energy proof for this form never needs the product rule.

The energy balance

Let q=WΦ be the grid function that the stencil acts on, and use the discrete energy E=12∑iHiwi(Πi2+Φi2). Then

dEdt =ΠTHWW−1Dq +ΦTHWDΠ =ΠTHDq+qTHDΠ =ΠTHDq−(Dq)THΠ =0

In the first step the factor W−1 from the equation cancels the weight in the energy, and ΦTHW=qTH because H and W are diagonal. The last step uses only HD=−DTH, summation by parts. Nothing is differentiated except q, and the singular coefficient b never appears.

Pointwise rule versus summed rule

This is the distinction that matters. The two forms need different properties of the stencil.

Integration by parts is a global identity: it only needs D to be antisymmetric in H, and a centred stencil with the right reflection rules is. The Leibniz rule is a local identity, and a stencil cannot satisfy it on data the grid barely resolves. The conservative form arranges the algebra so that only the first is used. This is the same statement as Rconservative=0 in step 2 of the previous subsection, since the conservative operator is W−1DW.

Why this removes the 1/h growth

The expanded form's growth came from b≈1/h multiplying a non-cancelling term at the first cells. In the conservative form b does not appear, so that mechanism is gone. The measured growth rate drops from about 0.39/h to about 0.2, which no longer grows as the grid is refined (see the table of growth rates above).

What it still misses: the origin node

The identity above requires the Π˙ equation to read W−1Dq at every node, including i=0. At that node w0=0, so the formula is 0/0 and the code substitutes the limit Π˙0=3Φ′. That has two consequences:

  • The balancing term H0Π0(Dq)0 that the cancellation needs is not what the energy sees. In the energy this node carries the weight H0w0=0, so the substituted equation contributes nothing.
  • Meanwhile Π0 still enters Φ˙1 and Φ˙2 through the stencil. That coupling has no partner term, so the cancellation fails there.

This is the unbalanced coupling at i=1 described in section 4, and it is why the conservative form still grows at rate 0.2 although the product rule is no longer the problem. The symmetrized form of section 4 removes it by absorbing the weight into the field: Θ=paΠ vanishes identically at the origin, so Θ0=0 and there is nothing left to couple. The derivation of this subsection is a hand calculation; I did not compute the node-0 residual numerically.

3. Symmetrized form (adsDivergenceExpanded, adsDPiExpanded)

The problem

The plain expanded equation

Π˙=Φ′+2bΦ Φ˙=Π′

is unstable with a centred stencil for every time step. The growth rate is about 0.39/h near x=π/2 and 0.1/h near x=0 (measured from the eigenvalues of the semi-discrete operator), so a finer grid blows up sooner. Rewriting bΦ with l'Hôpital near the ends lowers the rate near the origin but never removes it, and near π/2 it makes it worse.

The cure

Absorb a into the fields: Θ=aΠ, Ψ=aΦ. Since a′/a=b, the system becomes

Θ˙=Ψ′+bΨ Ψ˙=Θ′−bΘ

The b terms have opposite signs and cancel pointwise in ΘΨ˙+ΨΘ˙, so no product rule is needed. With the reflection rules used in derP, the two stencils satisfy summation by parts,

HDΘ+DΨTH=0,H=diag(12,1,…,1,12)

and E=∑iHii(Θi2+Ψi2) is conserved exactly by the semi-discrete system. Its eigenvalues are purely imaginary (largest real part 1e-14 for n=50…400, |λ|h=1.44, well inside the RK4 limit for Δt=h/2).

Going back to Π, Φ is a diagonal change of variables, which does not change the eigenvalues:

Π˙=(aΦ)′a+bΦ Φ˙=(aΠ)′a−bΠ

Expanding the first line gives Φ′+2bΦ and the second gives Π′, so this is the same equation. Only the discretization differs.

Implementation

Θ and Ψ are never stored. weightedStencil (in derivative.cpp) evaluates ajfj on the fly at the stencil points. This is the ordinary stencil with each neighbour weighted by a(xi+k)/a(xi). Ghost points use the parity of the product: a is odd at both ends, so aΦ is even at 0 and odd at π/2, and aΠ is the opposite. So Θ is odd at 0 and even at π/2, Ψ the opposite, and both vanish at the two end nodes.

Both equations must be used together, since the cancellation needs the pair.

Accuracy next to the origin

The division by ai≈ih multiplies the stencil error by 1/(ih). With the 5-point stencil of derP the operator was only O(h3) at the first grid points (2.4e-7 at i=1 for n=250, order 3.0). weightedStencil now uses a 7-point, 6th-order centred stencil with weights

(−160,320,−34,0,34,−320,160)/h

The operator error is O(h5) at the first points and O(h6) elsewhere (1e-10 at i=1 for n=250, down to the roundoff floor of about 1e-13 on finer grids). The pair is at least 4th order at every grid point, and the full evolution is limited by RK4 in time. Energy conservation survives because it only needs an antisymmetric centred stencil and the reflection rules at the ends, for any width (largest real part 1e-13 to 8e-12, |λ|h from 1.44 to 1.62). A wide stencil used only near the origin would break the symmetry, so the whole operator uses 7 points.

Cost

The weights call tan several times per point. On ./animation 2000 20 4 ads-expanded .01 it takes 84 s (74 s with the 5-point stencil, 25 s for the conservative ads form). Getting a(xi+kh) from a(xi) with the angle-addition formula halves the time of the 5-point version but changes the results by 3e-11 relative near π/2 (cancellation in 1−atan⁡h). That breaks the exact skew-symmetry, so it was not adopted.

4. Symmetrized right-hand side of ads and einstein

The problem with the conservative form

ads and einstein used to evaluate Π˙=(wmΦ)′/w in conservative form. That operator has an unstable mode at the first grid points. The origin node Π0 has energy weight w(0)=0 but still drives Φ1 and Φ2 through the stencil, and its own equation (3Φ′) is not the conjugate of those couplings. The discrete operator therefore cannot be skew in the energy weight.

It looks like physics. In long runs (n=500, σ=1/16) roundoff grew into a blow-up around t=150–300. In einstein mode this looks like a collapse (minA falling to 0.80 at t=250 from amplitude 0.01) before the run turns into NaN. It is invisible for t up to a few tens.

The cure with a weight m(x)

Let p=m and define Θ=paΠ, Ψ=paΦ. Then

Θ˙=SΨ+mbΨ Ψ˙=SΘ−mbΘ

where Sf=12(mf′+(mf)′) (called S in the README). S is skew-adjoint and the b terms cancel pointwise, so E=∫(Θ2+Ψ2)dx is conserved for fixed m. In the original variables this is a diagonal change:

Π˙=S(paΦ)pa+mbΦ=1w(wmΦ)′ Φ˙=S(paΠ)pa−mbΠ=(mΠ)′

Θ and Ψ are not stored: paf is evaluated on the fly at the 7 stencil points, with ghost points following the parity of the product. The origin node is decoupled (Θ0=0), which removes the unbalanced coupling.

Measured

  • No unstable eigenvalue: largest Reλ is 1e-13 to 7e-12 for n=50…400, for m=1, a bump, a steep dip, and a profile with an odd ρ3 part at π/2. |λ|h=1.62.
  • ads runs to T=400 with the inner value flat at 5e-6. einstein (amplitude 0.01) runs to T=400 with minA=1, where the old code blew up near t=200 and t=300.
  • The right-hand side is more accurate. For Π˙ at x<0.1 the error is order 4 (9.4e-10, 7.7e-11, 5.3e-12, 3.5e-13 for n=100…800), where the old one was order 2 (7.9e-6 to 1.2e-7). Elsewhere it is 10 to 100 times smaller. Φ˙ is order 3 at x<0.1, as before, set by the quadrature of A there.
  • The full coupled system converges at order 4.1 to 4.4 in L2 (test_convergence_einstein, amplitude 0.5). Collapse thresholds are unchanged (amplitude 30 regular, 60 collapses at the same step), and minA agrees with the old code to all printed digits.

adsDivergence and adsDPi (the conservative pair) are still in derivative.cpp and used by test_convergence_linear.cpp, which only compares resolutions up to T=π. Do not use them for long runs.

5. Differential operators and the symplectic structure

This section explains why the symmetrized pair works, in the language of Hamiltonian mechanics. The algebra in 5.1 to 5.3 is a derivation I checked by hand; 5.4 and 5.5 connect it to the measurements in the README. Nothing here was re-run in code.

5.1 Canonical structure

The radial action reduces to the Lagrangian density (up to the constant L2)

L=12w(φ˙2−φ′2)

with w=tan2x coming from −ggtt. The canonical momentum is p=wΠ, so the field and its momentum carry different weights. The energy and the symplectic form are

E[φ,p]=12∫(p2w+wφ′2)dx=12∫w(Π2+Φ2)dx Ω=∫δp∧δφdx=∫wδΠ∧δφdx

Hamilton's equations φ˙=δE/δp, p˙=−δE/δφ give φ˙=Π and p˙=(wφ′)′. Dividing the second by w and differentiating the first in x recovers exactly the system of section 1. The weight w vanishes at the origin and blows up at π/2, which is the root of every numerical difficulty above.

5.2 The two operators are adjoint to each other

Use the weighted inner product ⟨f,g⟩w=∫wfgdx. Integrating by parts (boundary terms vanish by the parities of section 1), the adjoint of ∂=d/dx is

∂†=−1w∂w

which is precisely the operator in the Π˙ equation. The system is therefore

Φ˙=∂Π Π˙=−∂†Φ

and eliminating Φ gives φ˙˙=−∂†∂φ. The operator ∂†∂ is symmetric and non-negative, so ω2≥0 for every mode and the energy E=12(‖Π‖w2+‖Φ‖w2) is conserved. The Poisson operator acting on u=(Π,Φ) is

u˙=JδEδu,J=0w−1∂∂w−10,δEδu=wu

J is skew-symmetric ((w−1∂)T=−∂w−1 in the plain L2 product) and has no dependence on the fields, so the Jacobi identity holds trivially. The flow therefore preserves both E and the symplectic form Ω.

The structural requirement. The discrete operator in the Π˙ equation must be minus the adjoint of the discrete operator in the Φ˙ equation, in the discrete energy inner product. This is a statement about the pair, not about either operator alone. It is what the expanded and conservative forms fail to satisfy, and what the next two subsections arrange by construction.

5.3 Symmetrized variables: the weight moves into the fields

With Θ=aΠ and Ψ=aΦ (so that a2=w), the weighted norm becomes the plain L2 norm, E=12∫(Θ2+Ψ2)dx, and the generator is

v˙=Kv,v=ΘΨ,K=0∂+b∂−b0

Because (∂+b)†=−∂+b=−(∂−b), the operator K is skew-adjoint in the plain inner product, and etK is unitary. Two features are worth naming:

  • The singular multiplier b appears only as an antisymmetric zeroth-order term. It rotates Θ into Ψ and never changes the norm, so its size near the ends does not matter for stability.
  • The Π, Φ form hides this: w−1∂w=∂+2b is a first-order operator whose zeroth-order part is a large symmetric term, the kind that can drive growth once the product rule fails.

In the (Θ,Ψ) variables the pair is not a canonical (q,p) pair. It is a Hamiltonian system in the Poisson form v˙=KδE/δv with δE/δv=v. The canonical pair remains (φ,wΠ).

5.4 The discrete operators

The discrete counterpart of "adjoint" is summation by parts. With the quadrature matrix H=diag(12,1,…,1,12)h (the trapezoid rule, which is where the half weights come from), the identity HDΘ+DΨTH=0 is the discrete form of (∂+b)†=−(∂−b). Then the semi-discrete system

v˙=Khv,KhTH=−HKh

is a finite-dimensional linear Hamiltonian system with constant skew Poisson matrix Jh=KhH−1 and energy 12vTHv. Its flow etKh preserves that energy and the symplectic structure exactly, and in the eigenbasis it is a set of independent oscillators. This is the reason the eigenvalues are purely imaginary: the largest real parts of 1e-13 to 7e-12 in the README are roundoff, not growth. The same eigenvalue test therefore doubles as a check that the discrete operators really are mutually adjoint.

What each discretization preserves
FormDiscrete adjoint relationOutcome
Expanded: Φ′+2bΦ Fails. The product rule defect is of order 1 on grid-scale modes, and the term 2b is symmetric. Growth rate about 0.39/h.
Conservative: D(wΦ)/w Fails at the origin. The node with w=0 has zero energy weight but is still coupled, so no weighted adjoint exists there. Growth rate about 0.2, a mode at i=1, visible after t≈150.

The same idea is known in the summation-by-parts literature as the skew-symmetric (split) form: write a variable-coefficient derivative as 12(m∂+∂m) rather than as either term alone. The operator S of section 4 is exactly that.

5.5 The normal modes and the period

For the massless scalar in AdS4 the normal-mode frequencies are the odd integers ωk=3+2k, k=0,1,2,… (a known result; I did not recompute the spectrum of the code). Since eiωkπ=−1 for every odd frequency, a pulse returns to its starting profile with the sign flipped after time π, and the field has period 2π. This matches the behaviour described in the README (framesPerCycle and the refocusing at t=π). Because the spectrum is exactly resonant, any frequency error of the discretization shows up as a phase drift, which is why the max-norm error grows with time even when the energy is conserved.

5.6 Time integration is not symplectic

Everything above concerns the semi-discrete system. RK4 is not a symplectic integrator. For one mode with z=iy, y=ωΔt, the amplification factor is R(z)=1+z+z22+z36+z424, and a direct expansion gives

|R(iy)|2=1−y672+y8576

So each step multiplies the energy of a mode by slightly less than one. The scheme is stable on the imaginary axis for |y|≤22≈2.83; the README reports |λ|h=1.62 at most, so with Δt=h/2 the largest y is 0.81, well inside.

  • Resolved modes: for y=0.025 the loss is y6/72≈3×10−12 per step. This is arithmetic from the formula above, not a measurement; the actual spectrum of the pulse was not computed.
  • Grid-scale modes: for y=0.81 the loss is about 0.4% of the energy per step, so RK4 acts as a weak selective filter on the worst-resolved modes.

The exact conservation of E mentioned in sections 3 and 4 is therefore a property of the spatial operator. The fully discrete energy decays very slowly. A structure-preserving integrator (implicit midpoint or Gauss-Legendre, which conserve quadratic invariants exactly, or a staggered leapfrog) would remove that decay at the price of a linear solve per step in the first case, or a different grid layout in the second. I have not tried either; RK4 was kept because its time error is already below the fourth-order spatial error.

5.7 With backreaction

For the coupled system the metric functions A and δ are not dynamical; they are recomputed from the constraints at every RK4 stage. Eliminating them leaves a reduced phase space with the same canonical pair (φ,p), p=wΠ (now Π=A−1eδφ˙), and the Hamiltonian is the total mass, not the quadratic form above. Two consequences for the code:

  • At each instant the operator is the weighted version of section 4, which is skew for the frozen m. This removes the spurious instability of the operator, and that is what the README measured (largest Reλ of 1e-13 to 7e-12 for several profiles of m).
  • ∫(Θ2+Ψ2)dx is not conserved, because m changes in time. The true invariant is the mass. The README does not test mass conservation of the scheme, only constraint residuals, convergence order and minA. The statement that the reduced dynamics is Hamiltonian with the mass as energy comes from the general theory of the spherically symmetric Einstein-scalar system, in the gauge δ(0)=0 the time is central proper time, so the generator differs from the boundary-time mass by a factor. I have not checked this in the code.

6. Einstein-scalar system with backreaction

The metric is ds2=L2cos2⁡x(−Ae−2δdt2+dx2A+sin2⁡xdΩ2), with Φ=φ′ and Π=A−1eδφ˙. Let s≡Φ2+Π2 (called S in the README).

Φ˙=(mΠ)′ Π˙=1w(wmΦ)′ δ′=−sin⁡xcos⁡xs,δ(0)=0 A′=1+2sin2⁡xsin⁡xcos⁡x(1−A)−sin⁡xcos⁡xAs,A(0)=1

Closed form for A and δ

A and δ are not stepped in time. At every RK4 stage they are recomputed from the current Π and Φ (solveGeometry):

δ(x)=−∫0xsin⁡ycos⁡ys(y)dy 1−A(x)=eδ(x)cos3⁡xsin⁡x∫0xe−δ(y)tan2⁡ys(y)dy

The quadrature is 4th-order cumulative. The integrand is smooth, including at π/2, and computing 1−A directly avoids cancellation and the 1/(sin⁡xcos⁡x) singularities. The time step uses evolveRK4(sf, &einsteinRHS). adsRHS is the same code with A=1 and δ=0 frozen, so one right-hand side serves both cases.

Checks

  • Inserting the computed A and δ into the differential equations: the δ′ residual is order 4, and the A′ residual is order 3 next to the origin (localized; it does not show in the evolution, which stays at order 4).
  • The sign is e−δ, not e+δ, for the given δ′ and A′. The wrong sign would have broken the constraints silently.

Collapse

The characteristic speed is m=Ae−δ. After every step the minimum of A is checked. If minA<1/n or A is not finite, the run stops with exit code 3. For strong fields Δt=h/2 is too large and the run goes to NaN a few steps before A actually reaches 0, so the reported collapse time is approximate.

8. Lessons learnt

Testing convergence

  • Compare resolutions at the same final time. The first test_convergence_point.cpp took one step per grid, so each resolution stopped at a different time and the ratio was exactly 2.0000. It measured how the solution changes with t, not the scheme order. Evolving every resolution to the same T gave 4.00.
  • Check the right-hand side before judging the scheme. An earlier version had Π˙=Π instead of Π˙=Φ′, so the spatial derivative never entered the test.
  • Know where the error floor is. At T=π the errors are about 1e-8 to 1e-14, near double precision, and an apparent order of 5 to 7 means nothing. A single point value can also change sign between resolutions. Prefer an L2 norm and a time where errors are well above roundoff.
  • Use an exact solution when there is one. For the flat wave equation the method of images gives one (test_boundary_flat.cpp).
  • The initial data limits the order. A nonzero Π′(0) gave about 1.2, and a wide pulse with a tail at the ends gave 0.8.

Equations and boundaries

  • The metric decides the equation. The first derivation assumed flat space and was wrong for this problem.
  • Conservative form is not enough; make the operator skew. Applying the stencil to wΦ and dividing afterwards looks stable for runs of a few tens of time units. But its discrete operator is not skew in the energy weight, which leaves a growing mode at i=1 that shows up only after t of 100 to 300. The symmetrized pair is what is needed (section 5 explains why). Check it with the eigenvalues of the operator.
  • Take the boundary parities from the asymptotics. With φ=fρ3+O(ρ5), Π is odd and Φ is even at x=π/2. The wrong parity gave first-order error near the outer boundary. derP takes a separate parity for each end.
  • Fill singular limits explicitly (Π˙=3Φ′ at the origin; extrapolation of wΦ at π/2).
  • Apply parity only to ghost points. The old stencil flipped the sign of real points too (<= instead of <). It was harmless only because those values were exactly zero.

Backreaction

  • Derive the sign from the metric (e−δ).
  • Solve the constraints in closed form with an integrating factor and an integration by parts.
  • Test an implementation against its own equations by inserting the computed A and δ into the differential equations.
  • Share one right-hand side. The linear case is A=1,δ=0, and the old behaviour was reproduced to about 1e-14.
  • A short run is not a stability test. An instability with Reλ=0.2 multiplies roundoff by about 1e16 only after t of roughly 180. Three earlier "stable" verdicts were based on T≤20. Compute the eigenvalues and run at least one long evolution (here T=400).

Robustness near collapse

  • The step limit changes with the fields. Treat the reported collapse time as approximate.
  • Stop on minA<1/n and on non-finite A. Check the initial data too.
  • Never write non-finite frames. On a collapse the output ends at the last finite state.
  • Validate arguments. stepsPerFrame = 0 crashed with a floating-point exception, and a tiny n read out of bounds.
  • Stability follows from structure, not from where you patch. Special treatment of the quotient near the ends (l'Hôpital, hybrids) only moved or reduced the growth. The cause was the broken product rule for the stencil, found by computing the eigenvalues of the linear operator as a matrix and measuring the defect. The fix was to change variables so the singular terms cancel pointwise.
  • Eigenvalues of the semi-discrete operator are the fastest diagnostic. Build the matrix from unit vectors and check maxReλ. An instability that scales like 1/h is the spatial discretization, not the time step, and where the eigenvector peaks says which end is responsible.

Output and plotting

  • Do not store more than can be seen. Cap the points per frame (256) and use 6 digits.
  • Make plot scripts robust. One frame with values around 1e300 made gnuplot's axis ranges infinite. plot.gp now ignores NaN/Inf and anything above cap.
  • Do not hard-code what the program can write. Frame times are written to times.dat.
  • Rendering time grows faster than the data, because each frame is looked up with index k.
  • GIF looping. animate loops forever by default, loop 0 also loops forever, loop 1 plays twice, and loop -1 plays once. Verified by reading the file's bytes.

Workflow

  • Read the file before describing it. Say what was and was not checked.
  • Rebuild after every source change. A stale animation produced a bad last frame; a build line without einstein.cpp fails to link.
  • Generated files can explode. A 3.8 GB NaN frames.dat appeared once. Binaries and output directories should not share a name.
  • Measure, then extrapolate. Where a number is a fit rather than a measurement, say so.
